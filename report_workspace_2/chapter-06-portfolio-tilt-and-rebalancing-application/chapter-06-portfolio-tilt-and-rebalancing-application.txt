CHAPTER 06
PORTFOLIO TILT AND REBALANCING APPLICATION

6.1 Introduction

This chapter converts the earlier statistical results into a practical KSE-30 application. Rather than stopping at fund-flow prediction, volatility estimation, and efficiency testing, the final project extends the analysis into a stock-level rebalancing framework. This is the most operational part of the dissertation because it asks how the extracted signals might help anticipate changes in KSE-30 membership and constituent weights.

The chapter is based mainly on `6_cursor_model/results_rebalancing.csv`, `6_cursor_model/results_rebalancing_forecast.csv`, and the rebalancing figures saved under `6_cursor_model/figures/rebalancing/`. The objective is not to claim that the pipeline replicates the exact official KSE-30 rule book in every detail. Instead, it builds a practical predictive framework that uses observable historical stock characteristics to estimate future inclusion and weight outcomes.

6.2 Motivation for Portfolio Tilt Using Fund-Flow Signals

The motivation for this chapter comes directly from the earlier findings. Chapter 5 showed that aggregate KSE-30 fund flows are not perfectly random, that volatility is persistent, and that return dynamics exhibit some evidence of serial dependence. If flows and market conditions contain usable information, then the next logical question is whether that information can support better positioning ahead of future KSE-30 rebalancing events.

In practical terms, index investors and fund managers do not need a perfect point forecast of monthly flow in PKR terms in order to benefit from the model. A more realistic use case is signal extraction. If the broader market environment and stock-level behaviour suggest that some names are likely to gain weight, retain index membership, or face exclusion risk, then portfolio weights can be tilted gradually before the next index review. This is the logic of the rebalancing block.

The chapter should therefore be framed as an application of the earlier empirical evidence, not as a disconnected new model family.

6.3 KSE-30 Rebalancing Framework

The final pipeline detects 17 rebalancing dates from the index history, spanning from 13 March 2020 to 16 March 2026. From these dates, a stock-level panel is built by taking pre-rebalancing feature snapshots and linking them to the next observed index outcome. After processing, the final panel contains 422 rows, 16 usable rebalancing windows, and 47 unique symbols.

The overall retention rate in this panel is 91.9 percent. This means most KSE-30 constituents remain in the index across adjacent review periods, while a smaller minority faces removal or replacement. This high persistence is economically intuitive for a major benchmark index, but it also creates a class-imbalance problem for inclusion prediction. That issue becomes important when interpreting the classification metrics later in the chapter.

The framework defines two separate tasks:

1. Weight prediction: estimate the next rebalancing-period constituent weight.
2. Inclusion and retention prediction: estimate whether the stock will remain in the KSE-30 at the next review.

This two-part structure is useful because rebalancing is not only about entry and exit. Even when a stock remains in the index, the size of its weight can still change in a way that matters for index-tracking portfolios.

6.4 Feature Construction for Rebalancing Prediction

The final rebalancing model uses a compact but economically interpretable feature set: current weight, 30-day, 60-day, and 90-day momentum, 30-day and 60-day volatility, average trading volume, market-cap proxy, price relative to 20-day and 50-day moving averages, weight drift, and weight range.

The statistical comparison between retained and dropped names shows that several features are meaningfully different across the two groups. Current weight is much higher for retained names at 3.8722 versus 1.3309 for dropped names, with a p-value effectively equal to zero. Retained names also have lower recent volatility on both the 30-day and 60-day measures, larger market-cap proxies, and wider historical weight ranges. These differences support the economic intuition that larger and more stable names are more likely to survive the next review.

By contrast, short-horizon momentum variables are not strongly significant in the retained-versus-dropped comparison. This suggests that index continuity in the KSE-30 is driven more by size and stability characteristics than by short-run performance alone.

6.5 Weight Prediction Models

The final pipeline compares three approaches for next-period weight prediction: a naive persistence benchmark, Ridge regression, and Random Forest regression. The naive model records RMSE = 0.5595, MAE = 0.2541, and R-squared = 0.9677. Ridge regression is almost identical in RMSE terms, with RMSE = 0.5590, MAE = 0.2963, and R-squared = 0.9678. Random Forest performs worse, with RMSE = 0.6900, MAE = 0.3553, and R-squared = 0.9509.

The interpretation of these results is important. The very strong naive benchmark already tells us that constituent weights are highly persistent across adjacent rebalancing windows. This is not a weakness of the exercise; it is itself a realistic feature of index composition. Ridge regression offers a very small improvement in RMSE and R-squared, which suggests that the added predictors capture some incremental information beyond simple carry-forward persistence. However, the gain is modest.

Random Forest does not improve on the linear benchmark in the final KSE-30 setup. This implies that, for this relatively small panel and fairly structured target, a simpler linear model is at least as reliable as a more flexible nonlinear model. The report should therefore describe Ridge as the preferred weight model, but also note that weight persistence is so strong that the naive baseline remains difficult to beat by a large margin.

6.6 Inclusion and Retention Prediction Models

For inclusion and retention, the final pipeline compares a naive "all retained" classifier, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest classification. All three models record the same test accuracy of 0.9655. At first glance, this seems to suggest no meaningful difference between them. However, accuracy is misleading here because the panel is highly imbalanced and the retention rate is already 91.9 percent.

The more informative metric is AUC. Logistic Regression achieves AUC = 0.8214, while Random Forest falls to 0.5446. The naive benchmark has no meaningful discriminatory AUC because it predicts retention for everyone. This means Logistic Regression is the most useful model in ranking stocks by retention probability, even though its raw accuracy is numerically identical to the naive benchmark.

The confusion-style counts printed by the pipeline also show why caution is necessary. In the test set, the models correctly classify most retained names, but there is little evidence of strong separation on true exclusion cases. Therefore, the correct interpretation is not that the classifier perfectly predicts removals. The stronger claim is narrower: Logistic Regression provides a more informative probability ranking than the naive rule, and that ranking can still be useful for portfolio tilting.

6.7 Forward Rebalancing Forecast

The final pipeline generates a forward-looking forecast for the next rebalancing window, approximately 16 September 2026. The forecast combines the average predicted weight from the Ridge and Random Forest models with the average retention probability from the Logistic and Random Forest classifiers. This produces a practical ranking of expected core names, likely weight gainers, and higher-risk constituents.

The largest predicted weights remain concentrated in established benchmark names. The top forecasted weights are FFC at 12.0857, UBL at 10.6149, ENGROH at 7.8589, MEBL at 5.7242, HUBC at 5.5357, and OGDC at 5.3005. This indicates that the model largely preserves the structure of the index and does not imply unrealistic turnover among dominant stocks.

At the same time, the forecast identifies a smaller set of names with elevated exclusion risk. The highest exclusion-risk stocks are SSGC at 52.89 percent, GAL at 39.59 percent, GHNI at 39.50 percent, PAEL at 33.78 percent, and BOP at 33.17 percent. These names should be interpreted as the most vulnerable positions in the next index review, not as certain removals.

The predicted weight changes are also informative. UBL shows the largest positive predicted change, rising by approximately 0.46 percentage points. POL and NBP also show moderate expected increases. On the downside, FFC is still forecast as the largest constituent but with an expected reduction of roughly 1.19 percentage points, while MEBL, HUBC, HBL, and several low-weight vulnerable names also show meaningful expected declines.

One technical caution should be stated in the report. A few low-weight names, such as SSGC and GAL, receive negative averaged regression weights in the raw forecast file. These should not be interpreted literally as feasible negative portfolio weights. They should instead be read as a signal of near-zero target weight and strong exclusion pressure in the next rebalance.

6.8 Practical Interpretation for Investors

The final rebalancing results suggest that the model is more useful as a ranking and tilting tool than as an exact replication engine for official index decisions. For an investor or fund manager, the most practical use is to identify three groups of stocks:

1. Core high-confidence holdings with large predicted weights and very high retention probability, such as FFC, UBL, MEBL, HUBC, OGDC, MCB, and MARI.
2. Moderate candidates for overweighting because of expected relative weight gains, such as UBL, POL, and NBP.
3. Vulnerable names that may warrant caution because of elevated exclusion risk or predicted weight compression, such as SSGC, GHNI, GAL, and PAEL.

This kind of signal can support gradual portfolio tilt ahead of the formal KSE-30 review rather than abrupt trading only after an official announcement. That said, the chapter should avoid overstating implementability. The final pipeline does not include a transaction-cost model, a live-trading backtest, or an out-of-sample portfolio performance attribution study. Therefore, the evidence supports a decision-support tool, not a fully validated trading strategy.

6.9 Chapter Summary

This chapter translated the earlier empirical findings into a practical KSE-30 rebalancing application. Using a panel of 422 stock-window observations across 16 rebalancing periods, the final pipeline predicted both next-period constituent weights and retention probabilities. Ridge regression emerged as the preferred weight model, though it only modestly improved on the already strong naive persistence benchmark. Logistic Regression emerged as the preferred inclusion model because its AUC exceeded both the naive rule and the Random Forest classifier.

The forward forecast indicates that the KSE-30 is likely to remain concentrated in established large-cap names, while a smaller group of low-weight stocks faces elevated exclusion risk. Overall, the rebalancing block shows that the project's earlier flow, volatility, and efficiency insights can be converted into a realistic index-tilt framework, even if the final output should be treated as a probabilistic aid to portfolio positioning rather than as an exact official index simulator.
